# Why a stone at the bottom of the sea was still money

- 시드: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rai_stones
- 매체 적합성: Fits — a 3.6-meter disc, a 1000km drift route, a stone going under, a stone that never moves while only its owner changes: the pictures carry the entire causal chain
- 핵심 질문: How could a stone nobody has ever seen again, sunk on the seafloor, go on being money?
- 핵심 반전: What made the value wasn't the stone but the agreement that the whole island remembered whose it was. So neither the stone's location nor the stone itself was needed
- 보이는 것: the Palau quarry → the 1000km current route → a stone sliding off a raft → the owner's name tag swapping over a stone fixed in place → gold in the Fed vault
- 차별점: A currency whose owner never once laid eyes on the object actually worked, and an economist named it as no different from our own financial system

## 대본

What if your money sank at sea and nobody lost a thing?
On Yap, in Micronesia, that actually happened.
Money here was a stone disc with a hole in it.
It's called rai. The biggest is 3.6 meters wide, 4 tons.
But Yap has no such stone.
The quarry was in Palau, 400 kilometers away.
Dozens of young men carved them, sometimes for years.
Rafts drifted out first; canoes chased them.
Men died on the way back. Stones sank.
So size alone never set a stone's value.
Who brought it, and who died for it, did.
Leave the stone, hand over the story, deal done.
The whole island was one living ledger.
So the stone that sank was still money.
Nobody saw it again, yet everyone agreed it was there.
Economist Milton Friedman said that's our story too.
Gold in the New York Fed's vault changes owners in place.
That stone on the seafloor wasn't lost. Its address was the sea.
