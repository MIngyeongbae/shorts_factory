# Edo graded its night soil — and then the speculators moved in

- 시드: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/下肥
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — the high/middle/low grade tags, the haul route from mansion to tenement to suburban field, the nutrient bars side by side, and the price curve that collapses are all pictures that carry the explanation
- 핵심 질문: Why did people in Edo pay money for other people's waste, and why did it come with grades?
- 핵심 반전: Price was set not by volume but by what the producer ate, and the collection right became a tradable, speculated-on asset — yet what broke the market was oversupply, not sanitation
- 보이는 것: money flowing in opposite directions between Europe and Japan; waste moving from daimyo mansion to tenement to suburban field; a grade table overlaid with nutrient bars; supply and demand curves crossing and inverting at the end
- 차별점: a city that assigned social rank to excrement and traded collection rights like property — and a market that collapsed on its own

## 대본

Europe paid to haul human waste away. Japan paid to buy it.
The missionary Fróis wrote it down, astonished, in 1585.
Nearby fields had no grass, no leaves. Only the city's waste.
But in Edo, night soil came graded: high, middle, low.
The grade depended on whose it was — on class.
Waste from households eating rich food and fish cost most.
Later, someone measured the chemistry. The hunch was right.
A soldier's held over twice the fruit-building phosphorus of a farmer's.
So a daimyo mansion's privy was treated like a small mine.
Uenoge village offered horses just to win the Ii estate.
Collection rights got bought and sold, then speculated on.
A tenement landlord's income came mostly from his tenants' privy.
Prices spiked, so 1,016 villages banded together and petitioned for cuts.
But as the city grew, the whole thing flipped.
The fields held only so much, and the supply kept climbing.
The overflow became a burden, not a product. Prices collapsed.
By Taishō, the city that once queued to buy paid to have it taken away.
