# The star of the container revolution wasn't the box — it was eight lumps of steel in its corners

- 시드: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_container
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — the oval holes in the corner castings, the cutaway of a lug entering and twisting shut, the 21-high stack cross-section, and the side-by-side with a swap body all show "why it stacks" directly
- 핵심 질문: They're identical-looking steel boxes, so why can some be stacked 21 high on a ship while others can't be stacked even once?
- 핵심 반전: The revolution wasn't the box — it was eight perforated steel castings set into its corners. The moment a crane's lug turns and locks inside one of those holes, the box stops being cargo and becomes a pillar holding up the boxes above it
- 보이는 것: rows of single-layer boxes from the era before standards → castings appearing on the top corners, then spreading to all eight → a cutaway of a lug turning and locking inside the oval hole → a stack climbing from the bottom of the hull to 21 high → a swap body with no castings on top
- 차별점: The swap body as counterexample — it looks exactly like a container, but with no top corner fittings it can neither be stacked nor lifted. The proof that the castings, not the box, are the story exists as a real object
- 영상 길이: About 70 seconds

## 대본

They say the container changed the world. But the box is just sheet steel.
The real stars are eight lumps of steel set into its corners.
Cargo boxes existed back in the 1930s. They just couldn't be stacked.
No stacking means one layer of boxes, and wasted space above them.
There wasn't even a standard place for a crane to grab.
In 1949, an engineer named Keith Tantlinger had the answer.
First, he set steel castings into the top corners.
A few years later, those castings spread to all eight corners.
And a single oval hole punched in them changes everything.
A crane's lug drops into that hole, turns once, and locks.
Like turning a doorknob to latch it. That's a twistlock.
The boxes above and below grip each other through that same steel.
That's the moment a box stops being cargo and becomes a pillar.
In 1957, the Gateway City was unloaded and reloaded in about eight hours.
Today the biggest ships stack 21 high from the bottom of the hull.
The proof is the swap body. It looks exactly like a container.
But most have no top corner fittings, so you can't stack or lift them.
Logistics wasn't changed by the box. It was those eight lumps of steel.
