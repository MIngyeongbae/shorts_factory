# Edo's firefighters smashed the untouched house next door

- 시드: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- 매체 적합성: Fits — draw the wind direction, the advancing fire line, and the demolition strip cut across its path on a map or floor plan, and the causation reads straight off the picture
- 핵심 질문: Why tear down the houses that weren't burning yet, and leave the burning one alone?
- 핵심 반전: With no water able to knock the fire down, putting it out meant removing the fuel first — so Edo's cheap, flimsy houses are thought to have saved the city, while the defense line seems to have broken at the big buildings nobody could pull down
- 보이는 것: a dense wooden city → flames riding the wind → houses collapsing ahead of the fire → the fire stopping at an empty strip → the strip breaking at a huge temple or mansion
- 차별점: a case where disaster response was designed as demolishing the city rather than extinguishing the fire, and the paradox of shoddy construction as a safety asset

## 대본

A house is burning, and the fire brigade tears down the one next door.
In Edo, Japan, firefighting meant demolition, not water.
Over 267 years, Edo had 49 great fires. Kyoto had 9.
Same country — so why did Edo burn like that?
Wooden houses packed tight, and a dry northwest wind every winter.
So why not just pour water on it? That wasn't an option.
The wooden hand pump, the ryudosui, only arrived in the 1760s.
Its stream was so weak it was useless against a real fire.
So the answer was demolition — take away the fire's food.
Crews pulled down the unburned houses ringing the fire.
An empty strip with nothing left to burn, and the fire starves.
The men doing it were construction workers, called tobi.
The same hands that built the houses were sent to wreck them.
And here, Edo's cheap flimsy row houses become a weapon.
They burned fast, but broke fast, so the line likely held in time.
Big solid temples and samurai mansions were harder to pull down.
Where the fire got past those, you had a great fire.
So in Edo, tearing down your neighbor's house was the fire hose.

- [Lines 1, 2] **Confirmed** — Edo's main means of fighting fire was not water but "hakai shobo," demolishing the surrounding buildings that had not yet caught — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Line 3] **Confirmed** — In the 267 years from 1601 (Keicho 6) to 1867 (Keio 3), Edo had 49 great fires; Kyoto had 9 in the same period (Osaka 6, Kanazawa 3) — 「火事の回数」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Line 4] **Confirmed** — Edo's fire frequency stood out against other major cities — 「火事の回数」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Line 5] **Confirmed** — Population growth pushed the townspeople's districts to extreme density, and the winter north/northwest karakkaze, an extremely dry strong wind, set the conditions for great fires — 「火事の原因 > 人口増加」「火事の原因 > 気象条件」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Lines 6, 8] **Confirmed** — Suppressing a fire with mass water discharge was impossible, and the ryudosui could not put out the volume needed either (about 15m of reach, hard to supply continuously) — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事 / 「火消」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/火消
- [Line 7] **Corrected (applied)** — The wooden hand pump ryudosui appeared around the Meiwa era; specifically, in 1764 (Meiwa 1) one unit was issued to each of the 13 machi-bikeshi companies. For the preceding 160-odd years there was no pump at all, so instead of "the ryudosui was all the pumping they had," the line now states when it was deployed — https://www.tfd.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/learning/elib/qa/qa_60.html
- [Lines 9, 10, 11] **Confirmed** — The main method of extinguishing fires was to demolish the not-yet-burning buildings around the fire, clear the combustibles, and create a firebreak to stop the spread — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Lines 12, 13] **Confirmed** — The core of the firefighting labor force (hikeshi ninsoku) was the tobi shokunin, craftsmen working in civil engineering and construction — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事 / 「江戸の火消」 https://www.tfd.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/learning/elib/times/times01.html
- [Lines 14, 15] **Leading hypothesis (softened to speculation)** — Some hold that flimsy cheap construction (yasubushin) fed the great fires, while structures easy to tear down and rebuild are also "thought to" have blocked the spread (「とも考えられ」) — not a settled consensus, so it is not stated as fact — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Line 16] **Leading hypothesis (softened to speculation)** — The seed does not state that large temples and samurai mansions "could not be torn down fast"; that causation is inferred by contrast with the easily demolished structures — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Line 17] **Confirmed** — At large temples and samurai mansions the spread could not be stopped, and the fires became great fires — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
- [Line 18] **Confirmed** — Until the Meiji Restoration, the mainstay of firefighting was demolition by the hikeshi labor crews — 「消防組織」 https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江戸の火事
