# Mount Fuji's summit has no address — how the top of the mountain became a shrine's land

- 시드: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富士山
- 매체 적합성: Good fit — draw the 8th-station line across a cross-section of the mountain and color above and below differently, and the split ownership lands in a single shot; the coins piling up in the crater, the court timeline, and a map with a blank address all hold the explanation up
- 핵심 질문: Why isn't the summit of Japan's national symbol public land?
- 핵심 반전: A money fight over who got to keep the coins thrown into the crater split the mountain by altitude, and that line survived all the way to the Supreme Court 200 years later

## 대본

Mount Fuji is Japan's national symbol, and its summit isn't public land.
The trail is cut into ten stations, and above the 8th it's almost all private property.
The owner is a shrine, Sengen Taisha. So how did that happen?
It started with a handful of coins thrown at the top of the mountain.
Pilgrims used to toss money down into the summit crater.
The right to collect that money was something the local lords handed out,
but each lord handed it to a different village, and a fight broke out.
Subashiri's share alone topped 76 ryo a year, about 1 ryo per household.
Fed up, Subashiri hauled Sengen Taisha into court.
The fight dragged on for 7 years, until the shogunate ruled on it itself.
In 1779, everything above the 8th station became Sengen Taisha's.
The mountain was sliced in layers like a cake, and only the top slice handed over.
Meiji seized the land, kept it after the war, and the shrine sued.
After a 17-year fight, in 1974, the court sided with the shrine.
And yet which prefecture this land sits in still hasn't been decided.
Yamanashi and Shizuoka agreed not to draw the border at all.
So Japan's most famous summit doesn't have an address.

- [Line 7] Because different lords granted the right to different villages, the claims overlapped, and Murayama, Subashiri and Ōmiya, who managed the trails, fought over it. The sums were large enough to make conflict likely — **Correction applied** ("finders keepers" removed) / §山役銭と内院散銭
- [Line 8] Subashiri village's share alone **exceeded** 76 ryo a year, working out to about 1 ryo per household — **Verified**, reflecting the original "越えた" / §山役銭と内院散銭 (source: 『小山町史 第2巻』)
- [Line 9] In 1772 (An'ei 1), Subashiri village sued Sengen Taisha, claiming control of the summit belonged to their village — **Verified** / §安永の争論
- [Line 10] The dispute ran 7 years, to 1779 (An'ei 8), and was settled by a ruling from the three commissioners — finance, city, and shrines-and-temples. The judgment reflected the shogunate's regard for Tokugawa Ieyasu's devotion to Sengen Taisha — **Verified** / §安永の争論
- [Line 11] The 1779 ruling assigned everything above Mount Fuji's 8th station to Fujisan Hongū Sengen Taisha (the ruling of the time carried no exceptions) — **Verified** / §安永の争論, §山頂部の所有
- [Line 12] The ownership boundary was drawn by station (altitude), not by terrain — **Verified (as metaphor)**. Note for art: station heights differ by trail, so the line is not perfectly horizontal
- [Line 13] Shrine and temple land was nationalized in the Meiji era; after the war it became subject to return, but land above the 8th station was kept as state land as "highly public-interest" land. **That** is why Sengen Taisha sued the state (about 86 years between nationalization and the suit) — **Correction applied** (direct cause → the postwar refusal to return it was the trigger) / §山頂部の所有
