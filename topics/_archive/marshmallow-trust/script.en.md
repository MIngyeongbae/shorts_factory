# The kid who ate the marshmallow first wasn't weak — they were quick at math

- 시드: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_marshmallow_experiment
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — an empty room, one marshmallow, a bell, a broken promise, and two bars of waiting time all prove visually what actually made a child wait
- 핵심 질문: Was the child who couldn't wait really the child with weak willpower?
- 핵심 반전: Kids who met an adult who broke a promise right before the test ate far sooner — waiting wasn't willpower, it was a judgment about whether the adult could be trusted
- 보이는 것: Same room, same marshmallow, same 15 minutes — only the adult in front of them changes, and the waiting-time bars stretch four times apart. Then the effect size shrinking once the sample gets ten times bigger
- 차별점: The original Stanford setup included a warm-up game where kids rang a bell to summon the adult — a trace that the designers already knew trust was a variable
- 여운: What did the kid who ate first actually see?

## 대본

Everything you've been told about the Marshmallow Test is a lie.
1970s Stanford: one marshmallow, one four-year-old.
Wait 15 minutes and you get two. Then the adult walked out.
Some ate right away. Some sang to themselves and held on.
Over a decade later, the kids who waited had higher SAT scores.
The world decided: willpower is what shapes your life.
Then in 2012, the University of Rochester flipped the order.
Before the marshmallow, an adult made the child a small promise.
For one group they kept it. For the other, they broke it, openly.
Kids who met the adult who kept their word waited four times longer.
The child wasn't being tested on patience. The child was doing math.
In front of an adult who breaks promises, waiting is a losing bet.
If the bank looks like it's closing, you take cash over deposits.
At Stanford too, kids first rang a bell to call the adult back.
Only after "ring, and they come" did the real test begin.
A 2018 replication cut the effect in half,
and the other half wasn't willpower — it was family income.
The kid who ate first wasn't weaker. They just trusted less.

- [줄 3] The child could choose between one small reward given immediately and two rewards after waiting about 15 minutes, while the experimenter left the room — seed lead and "Original Stanford experiment" section [Verified]
- [줄 4] Observations record waiting children making up songs, covering their eyes, and otherwise distracting themselves — quoted passage in the seed's "Original Stanford experiment" section [Verified]
- [줄 5] A 1990 follow-up found that children who waited longer correlated with higher SAT scores (Shoda, Mischel & Peake, 1990). The first 1988 follow-up was an assessment "more than 10 years later" — seed "Follow-up studies" section [Verified]
- [줄 6] Coverage of that correlation hardened into the popular belief that willpower decides your life (SAT scores, educational attainment, BMI) — seed lead [Verified]
- [줄 7] In 2012 the University of Rochester ran a modified marshmallow test (N=28, ages 3;6–5;10, mean 4;6) — seed "Follow-up studies" section / Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin, Cognition (2013) [Verified]
- [줄 8–9] Before the test, one group met an experimenter who broke a promise (unreliable tester) and the other met one who kept it (reliable tester) — seed "Follow-up studies" section [Verified]
- [줄 10] The group that met the reliable tester waited up to four times longer (about 12 minutes) than the other. The paper's measured means were 12 min 02 s vs 3 min 02 s, and the share who lasted the full 15 minutes was 9/14 (64.3%) vs 1/14 (7.1%) — seed "Follow-up studies" section / https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3730121/ [Verified]
- [줄 11–12] The authors argued this calls the original self-control interpretation into question — self-control should predict the ability to wait, not strategic waiting that happens only when waiting pays off — seed "Follow-up studies" section [Verified]
- [줄 14–15] In the Stanford experiment, to build trust that the experimenter would return, children first played a game in which they summoned the experimenter by ringing a bell, and the actual waiting period did not start until the children clearly understood the arrangement — seed "Follow-up studies" section [Verified]
- [줄 14–15 보조] Before the Stanford experiment, Mischel had already shown that a child's belief that the promised delayed reward would actually arrive was a key determinant of the choice to wait — seed "Follow-up studies" section (Mischel 1961, 1966; Mischel & Staub 1965; Mischel & Grusec 1967) [Verified]
- [줄 16] A replication more than 10 times larger and drawn from a more diverse population (Watts, Duncan & Quan, 2018; N=918) found an effect size about half that of the original — seed lead / https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6050075/ [Verified]
- [줄 17] That replication suggested the other half is explained by economic background rather than willpower, and the effect shrank by two thirds once early cognitive ability, family background, and home environment were controlled for — seed lead (economic background sentence) / https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6050075/ [Verified]
- [줄 18] The closing claim — not weaker, just less trusting — matches Kidd et al.'s environmental-reliability interpretation — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3730121/ [Verified]
