# Hoover Dam — poured in one piece, it would have spent 125 years cooling

- 시드: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Dam
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — the grid of block columns, the pipes running through them, the heat leaving through those pipes: every step is something you can show on screen
- 핵심 질문: How do you cool a mountain of concrete without letting it crack?
- 핵심 반전: Concrete doesn't harden by drying — it hardens by giving off heat. Poured as one mass, it would take 125 years to cool and tear itself apart on the way
- 보이는 것: Rectangles chalked onto the canyon floor → block columns rising layer by layer → ice water running through steel pipe in every layer, carrying heat out → the pipes filled at the end, fusing it all into one mass
- 차별점: Hoover Dam seen not as "the biggest" but as a cooling problem — more than 937 kilometers of pipe and an on-site plant making ice water

## 대본

If Hoover Dam had been poured all at once, it would still be cooling today
125 years to cool — that was the math done by the engineers who designed it
Concrete doesn't harden by drying out; it gives off heat as it cures
The outside cools and shrinks first, while the core is still hot
When outside and inside shrink at different rates, the seam between them tears
And if the dam split, the towns downstream would be swept away
So the engineers never poured the dam as a single mass
They chalked rectangles on the floor and stacked blocks like columns
Every block was cooled on its own, and allowed to shrink on its own
Into each layer they packed steel pipes 2.5 centimeters across
First cold river water, then ice water from a refrigeration plant
Veins threaded through a mountain of concrete, drawing the heat out
Add up all that pipe and it comes to more than 937 kilometers
Once a block was cool, they filled its pipes with cement and locked it solid
More than two years ahead of contract, the dam passed to the government
So what became of the concrete that nearly waited 125 years?
90 years on, it's still hardening — very, very slowly
