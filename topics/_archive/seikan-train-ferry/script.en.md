# The Seikan ferry — why a ship swallowed a whole train

- 시드: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/青函連絡船
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — one unbroken line of rail running off the land, across a movable bridge, into the belly of a ship, and a hull that sinks lower with every freight car; it all explains itself on screen
- 핵심 질문: If you could just move the cargo, why push the entire train onto a ship?
- 핵심 반전: The bottleneck was never the sea — it was the loading at the pier. Making the ship one segment of the railway erased the loading itself, and the price was a large hole left in the ship's stern
- 보이는 것: the movable bridge on the quay following the tilt of the hull, freight cars pushed in through the stern, the watertight door that sealed that stern after the disaster, and finally a train running under the strait
- 차별점: the order of events — a coupler standard had to be solved before any ship could work — and the closed loop in which the disaster produced the tunnel that finally ended the ferry

## 대본

Why not just move the cargo? Why push a whole train onto a ship?
Aomori, at the tip of Honshu, and Hakodate in Hokkaido — the Seikan ferry.
Four hours by sea. But the time was leaking away at the pier.
Barges moved every load piece by piece, and freight piled up at the port.
So they flipped the idea. Not the cargo — the freight car itself.
But Honshu and Hokkaido used different coupler standards.
In 1925, Honshu replaced its standard, and the way opened.
On the quay, a movable bridge — the joint between land rails and ship.
The more cars rolled on, the lower the ship sat, and the bridge tilted with it.
So the freight was never set down, not even once.
At the peak, the ships made 30 round trips across the strait in a day.
But there's a price. For a train to get in, the stern has to be open.
In the autumn of 1954, a huge typhoon swept over the strait.
Five ferries went down. 1,430 people were lost.
After that, ships carried watertight stern doors, and an undersea tunnel became real.
On March 13, 1988, the tunnel opened and regular service came to an end.
Today trains aren't loaded onto ships. They cross under the sea on their own.

## 주장

- [줄 1·5·10] **확인** — Instead of transferring cargo, the Seikan ferry carried freight cars and baggage cars directly aboard (車両航送), a dramatic gain in efficiency over the earlier barge handling — 「歴史」·「年表 1925年8月1日」
- [줄 2] **확인** — The route was a railway ferry linking Aomori Station, the northern end of Honshu's trunk line, with Hakodate Station at the southern end of Hokkaido — opening of the article
- [줄 3] **확인** — Opened March 7, 1908; the steam turbine ship Hirafu Maru connected the two ports in four hours (figure as of opening) — 「歴史 1908年」
- [줄 3·4] **확인** — At opening neither port had a quay a ferry could berth at, so passengers and cargo moved by small steamers and barges (hashike); the system could not absorb the surge in freight demand during the First World War and fell into chaos. That freight chaos is what triggered the start of railcar ferrying — 「運行区間 > 桟橋 > 青森桟橋·函館桟橋」
- [줄 5] **확인** — Trial railcar ferrying on May 21, 1925; regular service from August 1 the same year — 「年表 1925年」
- [줄 6·7] **확인** — On July 17, 1925, the mass conversion to automatic couplers south of Honshu was completed, unifying couplers on cars inside and outside Hokkaido and making through operation possible (Hokkaido already used automatic couplers; Honshu and south used screw couplings) — 「運行区間 > 桟橋 > 青森桟橋」·「連結器」
- [줄 8·9] **확인** — A movable bridge on the quay joined the rails to the ship, and a new type was used with a flexible auxiliary girder that followed the hull's tilt smoothly — 「運行区間 > 桟橋 > 青森桟橋」
- [줄 11] **확인** — The peak came in 1972 with a maximum of 30 round trips a day — 「最盛期」·「年表 1972年」
- [줄 12] **확인** — To take railcars aboard, the stern was an opening one track wide, with no stern door — 「洞爺丸事故」
- [줄 13·14] **확인** — On September 26, 1954, the winds and waves of Typhoon No. 15 (the Toya Maru typhoon) sank five ferries outside the breakwater of Hakodate port; 1,430 people died (1,155 on the Toya Maru alone) — 「年表 1954年」·「洞爺丸事故」
- [줄 15] **정정** — New ships did not all receive stern doors right after the disaster. The first Hiyama Maru, entering service in 1955, had no stern door and instead carried 40 drainage openings along both sides of the car deck's stern, while her sister ship Sorachi Maru did have a vertically sliding stern door. Watertight stern doors spread as one of the post-disaster improvements, through new construction and refits → revised to "ships carried watertight stern doors" — 「檜山丸（初代）」·「洞爺丸事故」
- [줄 15] **확인** — The disaster ended the ferrying of revenue passenger cars (mail and baggage cars excepted) and turned the Seikan Tunnel plan into a concrete project — 「年表 1954年9月26日」·「洞爺丸事故」
- [줄 16] **정정** — On March 13, 1988, the Kaikyo Line including the Seikan Tunnel opened and **regular service** ended that same day; a provisional daytime service of two round trips a day ran from June 3 to September 18 that year → revised to "regular service came to an end" — 「青函トンネル開通・青函連絡船の終焉とその後」
- [줄 17] **확인** — The Aomori–Hakodate link passed to the Seikan Tunnel — 「青函トンネル開通・青函連絡船の終焉とその後」
