# Ondol — a heater that filled one or two rooms, and stripped Joseon's mountains bare

- 시드: https://namu.wiki/w/온돌
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — the path of heat spreading from the firebox to the flue stones, the area of a one-person kang laid over a whole ondol floor, the pavilion skyline of Kaesong flattening into a one-story town, the map of forest retreating until only the Kaema Plateau and the Baekdudaegan are left: every beat is a picture that pushes the understanding forward
- 핵심 질문: What did the choice to heat an entire floor end up costing an entire country?
- 핵심 반전: Ondol began as heating for one or two rooms — the old and the sick — even in noble houses, and only after the Little Ice Age pushed it into every class did it eat away both the forests and the number of stories a building could have
- 보이는 것: The kang that warms only a sleeping platform against the ondol that warms the whole floor, a map of mountains going bald as fuel demand swells, a city silhouette losing its pavilions as roofs drop
- 차별점: One heating method dragging fuel, forests, building height, and floor-sitting life along behind it in a single chain

## 대본

In early Joseon, even a noble's mansion had ondol in just one or two rooms.
The rooms of the old and the sick. Warm floors stopped there.
Then, in the 17th century, the Little Ice Age closes in on Joseon.
Even palace maids gave up their wood-floored rooms for ondol.
In Manchuria, China, a kang is a brick bed that warms only where you sleep.
With ondol, the whole floor is that bed — so it eats far more wood.
By the middle of the 18th century, demand for ondol peaked.
Under King Jeongjo, wood per person was 9% of the founding level.
Illegal logging spread, and villages bolted their own mountains shut.
In 1788, the court even issued a law to protect pine trees.
But the axes moved faster than the rules did.
By 1910, only 21% of the land was still real forest.
Forest survived only in far places — the Kaema Plateau, the Baekdudaegan.
And mountains weren't the only thing ondol cut down.
In Goryeo's Kaesong, every few dozen houses had a pavilion looking out.
Laying a gudeul means a heavy structure packed under the floor.
So Joseon's houses gave up their upper stories and pressed themselves flat.
That two-room ondol became a country's floor, paid for in forest and storeys.
