# Solar salt — the salt Koreans call "traditional" is a 1907 import

- 시드: https://namu.wiki/w/천일염
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — the changing saltern floor (earth → tile → vinyl sheeting), the rainfall-concentration figures, and a map sliding from Juan in Incheon down to Sinan all show the causality on screen
- 핵심 질문: If the coarse salt that goes into kimchi isn't traditional, how did the whole country end up using it?
- 핵심 반전: Koreans didn't skip solar salt because they'd never heard of it — the climate seems to be why, since the rain comes down exactly in salt-making season. And the reason they adopted it anyway was that the firewood for boiling had run out
- 보이는 것: woodpiles beside a jayeom cauldron → stripped bare hills → the 1907 Juan saltern → the northern salterns cut away by partition (4,261 of 5,925 jeongbo) → tile and vinyl going down on Sinan's soft mudflats
- 차별점: the record that Koreans looked down on this salt as waeyeom, "Japanese salt" — a salt that started from the exact opposite of tradition

## 대본

That coarse salt in your kimchi? It isn't a tradition. It's an import.
Korea's traditional salt was jayeom — seawater boiled down in cauldrons.
Solar salt starts in 1907, at the Juan saltern in Incheon.
Japan brought over the Taiwanese method it won in the Sino-Japanese War.
So had Koreans back then just never thought of the easy way?
They knew. They seem to have passed. The sky wasn't cooperating.
Solar salt needs a long stretch with the rain switched off.
In Korea, 54% of the year's rain falls in salt-making summer.
It was like hanging the laundry out in monsoon season.
They pushed it through anyway, because there was no wood left to burn.
Jayeom eats firewood, and the hills were already stripped bare.
Koreans themselves sneered at it as waeyeom — Japanese salt.
The war flipped the board. Seven of every ten salterns were up north.
What was left was Sinan's soft mudflats, and they laid salterns there anyway.
Mud clouded the brine, so down went tile, then vinyl sheeting.
70 years on, the imported method is sitting in tradition's seat.
So that coarse salt isn't tradition. It's what circumstance left behind.

## 주장

- [줄 1] Solar salt is the coarse salt used for kimchi and jang, and it is not a Korean traditional production method but one that arrived in the last years of the Korean Empire — **Confirmed** ([Jeollanam-do Province](https://solarsalt.jeonnam.go.kr/main/cm/cntnts/cntntsView.do?mi=9102&cntntsId=65), [CJ Newsroom](https://cjnews.cj.net/천일염-꽃소금-맛소금-다른-점이-뭐야/))
- [줄 2] The traditional salt of the Korean peninsula is jayeom, made by boiling seawater in cauldrons — **Confirmed** ([우리역사넷](https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/km/view.do?levelId=km_012_0070_0030_0020), [Nongmin Shinmun](https://www.nongmin.com/article/20120819033415))
- [줄 3] Solar salt begins with the **Taiwanese-style experimental solar saltern (1 jeongbo)** built at Juan in Incheon in 1907 — **Confirmed** ([Michuhol Culture Encyclopedia](http://www.grandculture.net/michuhol/toc/GC04700852), [Jeollanam-do Province](https://solarsalt.jeonnam.go.kr/main/cm/cntnts/cntntsView.do?mi=9102&cntntsId=65)) / *Correction: "the first tile-floored saltern" is wrong — tile floors came much later*
- [줄 4] In 1906 an engineer from the Taiwan Government-General's monopoly bureau surveyed the tidal flats, and in 1907 the Ministry of Finance (under the Residency-General) built a Taiwanese-style solar saltworks at Juan. Taiwan had been ceded to Japan by the Treaty of Shimonoseki, the outcome of the Sino-Japanese War — **Confirmed** ([Michuhol Culture Encyclopedia](http://www.grandculture.net/michuhol/toc/GC04700852), [Treaty of Shimonoseki](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/시모노세키_조약))
- [줄 5~6] Koreans boiled their salt not because the solar method was unknown to them but because the climate did not suit it — **Probable** (no source beyond the originating Namuwiki entry) / *Correction: line 6 hedged to "they seem to have passed"*
- [줄 7] Solar salt requires a long dry spell with no rain — **Confirmed** (the principle of evaporative concentration and the shared condition of solar salt regions worldwide) / *Correction: "annual mean above 25℃ · Mediterranean climate (France, Spain)" is **Unsupported** — Guérande has a Cfb oceanic climate ([source](https://www.whereandwhen.net/when/europe/pays-de-la-loire/guerande/)), and the top producing countries are China, the US, India, Australia and Mexico ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-salt.pdf)) → deleted*
- [줄 8] Of Korea's 1,306.3mm of annual rainfall, 710.9mm (**54%**) falls in summer, so the salt-making season overlaps the wet season — **Confirmed** ([Korea Meteorological Administration](https://www.weather.go.kr/w/climate/statistics/korea.do)) / *Correction: "annual mean temperature 15℃" is wrong — the national normal is **12.8℃** (7–15℃ by region) ([KMA normals](https://data.kma.go.kr/normals/index.do)) → temperature figure deleted. "Evaporation of 1,200–1,400mm" and "seawater below 2% concentration" are **Unsupported** → unused*
- [줄 10] The Joseon government and the Government-General promoted solar salt because firewood had become hard to obtain — **Confirmed** ([Newsvision e](http://www.nvp.co.kr/news/articleView.html?idxno=309786), [National Archives of Korea](https://theme.archives.go.kr/next/forest/background/immediateCausesOfDeforestation.do))
- [줄 11] Jayeom consumes large amounts of firewood, and the forests were already devastated (only 21% of forest intact in 1910) — **Probable→Corrected** ([National Archives of Korea](https://theme.archives.go.kr/next/forest/background/processAndCausesOfDeforestation.do)) / *Correction: the leading direct cause of bare hills was gathering fuel for ondol heating, so "jayeom stripped the hills bare" was changed to "the hills were already stripped bare"*
- [줄 12] Solar salt was called waeyeom, meaning "the salt the Japanese brought" — **Confirmed** ([Newsvision e](http://www.nvp.co.kr/news/articleView.html?idxno=309786)) / *Correction: "scorned for its bitterness" is **Probable** → dropped in favor of the confirmed etymology*
- [줄 13] In 1945, 4,261 of the 5,925 jeongbo of state-run solar salterns (**71.9%**) lay in the north, leaving the south only 1,664 jeongbo at Juan, Sorae, Namdong, Gunja and elsewhere. The 1,250-jeongbo Haenam saltern built at Yeonbaek in 1943 also ended up above the armistice line — **Confirmed** ([Incheon Ilbo](http://www.incheonilbo.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=1040699)) / *Correction: "firm tidal flats existed only in Pyeongan, Hwanghae and Gyeonggi" is **Unsupported** → replaced with the verified distribution ("seven of every ten")*
- [줄 14] So salterns were built across Sinan in South Jeolla, with its broad muddy tidal flats, and the postwar five-year salt production plan (1952–56) opened up the southwest coast on a large scale — **Confirmed** ([Getbol, Sinan](https://www.getbolworldheritage.org/k_getbol/shinan), [Incheon Ilbo](http://www.incheonilbo.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=1040699)) / *Note: Sinan's first saltern was at Bigeum-myeon in 1947 — the war is what detonated the expansion ([Shinan County](https://www.shinan.go.kr/home/www/dept_info/economy/salt_info/salt_info_10/page.wscms))*
- [줄 15] Saltern floors went from bare earth to pottery shards to ceramic tile to PVC sheeting, and sheet-floor salt has been dominant since the 1980s (about 90% today) — **Confirmed** ([Patent KR20150006989A](https://patents.google.com/patent/KR20150006989A/ko), [Le Monde diplomatique](https://www.ilemonde.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=14882))
- [줄 16] Korea first met its own demand in 1955 with 350,000 tons, roughly 70 years ago — **Confirmed** ([Jeollanam-do Province](https://solarsalt.jeonnam.go.kr/main/cm/cntnts/cntntsView.do?mi=9102&cntntsId=65))
- [줄 17] The spread of solar salt was not the passing down of a tradition but the result of fuel, partition and climate — **Confirmed** (all of the above taken together)
