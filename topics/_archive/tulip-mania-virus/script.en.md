# Tulip mania — the story that it wrecked the economy is false

- 시드: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_mania
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — put a solid-coloured tulip beside a flamed one, point out on screen that the flame is where a virus split the colour, then follow a winter in which only contracts move while the flowers stay in the ground, and the price line snapping vertically in February 1637; the whole explanation resolves in pictures
- 핵심 질문: Why was the flower that cost more than a house a diseased flower — and why did nobody go broke when its price collapsed?
- 핵심 반전: The pattern that made the price was the mark of the tulip breaking virus, and the same virus wrecked daughter-bulb production, manufacturing the scarcity itself. And what crashed was the price written on the contracts, not money that had changed hands — the "Holland was ruined" story hardened in 1841, when Mackay, by way of Beckmann (1797), copied satire down as fact
- 보이는 것: petals splitting from one colour into two (red flames on white), daughter bulbs dwindling year by year, winter contracts passing hand to hand while the flower stays underground, the port of Amsterdam running normally beside the snapping price line
- 차별점: not a story about bubble psychology, but an event where one plant disease and one sheet of paper made the price — plus the documentary evidence that not a single tulip bankruptcy has ever been identified, and fewer than six people even hit financial trouble
- 여운: What scarcity are we buying?

## 대본

A single tulip bulb once cost as much as a house.
The Netherlands, 1630s — the bulbs whose prices soared were the striped ones.
White petals streaked with red flame, a pattern nobody could make on purpose.
And that pattern turned out to be, of all things, a virus.
The tulip breaking virus was splitting one petal colour into two.
But the same disease also ate away at the plant's daughter bulbs.
The brush that painted the pattern had smashed the copier too.
The rarer the pattern, the fewer there were, and prices kept climbing.
Bulbs could only be dug up in summer, so winter traded nothing but paper.
That's why the Dutch called it windhandel — the wind trade.
In February 1637, the buyers seem to have dried up, and prices broke.
But when historian Anne Goldgar dug through the surviving contracts,
she found fewer than six people in any financial trouble at all.
No money had actually changed hands, so there was nothing to lose.
Then where does the story about a ruined nation come from?
In 1841, Charles Mackay copied a satirical pamphlet down as fact.
The Dutch economy stayed the world's richest for over 80 years after that.
What collapsed wasn't an economy. It was the price of one sick flower.

## 주장

- [줄 1] The claim that a tulip bulb cost as much as a house holds up. A Viceroy bulb was worth upwards of five times the average house price of the day (about ƒ2,500), and the highest-priced contract Goldgar found, roughly 5,000 guilders, was the price of "a well-appointed house". **The most expensive variety was not the Viceroy but the Semper Augustus (up to ƒ10,000)** — 시드 「Modern views」, 리드 / 『The Conversation』(Goldgar) 〈확인, 품종 서술 정정〉
- [줄 2] The surge ran from 1634 to February 1637, and the bulbs that rose first were the broken (striped) ones; professional growers paid higher and higher prices for virus-infected bulbs — 「Speculative period」, 인포박스 〈확인〉
- [줄 3] The cause of the patterning was unknown at the time (traced to an aphid-borne disease by Dorothy Cayley in 1928, confirmed as a mosaic virus in the 1960s), naming was haphazard, and quality varied widely — i.e. a desired pattern could not be reproduced on purpose — 「The Dutch tulip business」, 암스테르담 튤립 박물관 〈확인〉
- [줄 3] The standard form of the break is **red or purple flames on a white (or yellow) ground**. The Semper Augustus is recorded as "white, with carmine", and the three classes of broken tulip are likewise Roses = red to pink on white, Bijbloemens = purple on white, Bizarres = brown, purple or red on yellow — 시드, 암스테르담 튤립 박물관 〈정정: "붉은 바탕에 흰 불꽃" → "흰 바탕에 붉은 불꽃"〉
- [줄 4–5] The breaking is caused by infection with a tulip-specific mosaic virus, the "tulip breaking virus", so named because it "breaks" one petal colour into two or more — 「The Dutch tulip business」 〈확인〉
- [줄 6] The same virus progressively impairs the tulip's production of daughter bulbs, and the bulbs grow smaller with each generation — 「The Dutch tulip business」, 「Tulip breaking virus」 〈확인〉
- [줄 8] Because propagation was suppressed, supply of the patterned varieties could not grow, and even after prices rose growers had no chance to increase that year's production (the rise came after the bulbs were already planted). A broken tulip was "more likely to die out than to multiply" — 「The Dutch tulip business」, 「Natural volatility in flower prices」, 암스테르담 튤립 박물관 〈확인〉
- [줄 9] Bulbs could only be lifted and moved during the June–September dormancy, so spot purchases happened in those months while the rest of the year ran on forward contracts signed before a notary (the script's "in summer" compresses that June–September window) — 「The Dutch tulip business」 〈확인〉
- [줄 10] The Dutch called this contract trading windhandel ("wind trade"), because no bulbs actually changed hands (the seed renders it 'air trade') — 「Speculative period」 〈확인〉
- [줄 11] Bulb contract prices collapsed abruptly in February 1637, apparently by the end of the first week of the month — 「Speculative period」 〈확인〉
- [줄 11] That the trigger was buyers running out is not established. The story of an auctioneer at Haarlem failing to find willing buyers on 3 February rests on **a contemporary satire**, and the precise circumstances are unknown — 「Speculative period」, 「Mackay's Madness of Crowds」 〈유력설: 해당 줄을 "무너진 듯합니다"로 추정 표현화〉
- [줄 12–13] Historian Anne Goldgar's search of the surviving contracts found fewer than half a dozen people who ran into financial trouble, and even for those it is unclear that tulips were to blame; not a single bankrupt could be identified as felled by tulip mania — 「Modern views」(Goldgar 2007), 『The Conversation』 〈확인〉
- [줄 14] Prices had risen, but money had not actually changed hands between buyers and sellers, so the collapse in prices itself caused no losses (buyers who had bought on credit expecting a profit are the exception) — 「Modern views」 〈확인〉
- [줄 16] In 1841 the Scottish journalist Charles Mackay covered the episode in Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds, drawing, by way of Johann Beckmann's 1797 work, heavily on the satirical Dialogues (Samenspraeck tusschen Waermondt ende Gaergoedt) written to mock the speculators. Economic historians have debunked much of it since the 1980s — 「Mackay's Madness of Crowds」 〈확인, 전달 경로는 한 단계 간접〉
- [줄 17] Tulip mania had no critical influence on the prosperity of the Dutch Republic, which held the highest per capita income in the world from circa 1600 to 1720 — about **83 years** after the 1637 crash, so "more than 80 years" is accurate and "a century" is not — 리드 문단, 각주 5(Kaletsky 2010) 〈정정: "한 세기 동안" → "80년 넘게"〉
