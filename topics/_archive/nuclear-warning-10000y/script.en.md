# How to tell someone 10,000 years from now, "don't dig here"

- 시드: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-term_nuclear_waste_warning_messages
- 매체 적합성: Strong fit — a field of stone spikes, a maze of house-sized black blocks, a building that whistles, cats that change color: every one is a real design proposal, so the visuals do the explaining
- 핵심 질문: How do you write a warning that still reads after every script we use today is gone
- 핵심 반전: Monuments exist to be remembered, but this one has to carve "nothing of value is here." The answer moves from a sentence to terror, and then to a song
- 보이는 것: Words cut into stone → landscape design that turns the whole site into a weapon → a living cat and a chorus, as the container for the message keeps changing
- 차별점: It's a nuclear waste problem, but the people called in weren't engineers — they were linguists, and what came out wasn't a report, it was a song

## 대본

How do you tell someone 10,000 years from now, "don't dig here"?
The waste America buried under the desert stays deadly 10,000 years.
But language is clothing we keep changing — by then, nobody reads ours.
So in 1981, the U.S. brought in linguists.
In 1993, Sandia National Laboratories came back with sentences like these.
"This place is not a place of honor. No esteemed deed is commemorated here."
"What is here was dangerous and repulsive to us. This is a warning."
But if writing can't be trusted, make the land itself frightening.
Stone spikes 21 meters tall, driven into the ground at a lean.
Hundreds of house-sized black blocks, forming streets that lead nowhere.
A building shaped so the wind through it whistles, mournful and off-key.
The stranger proposal, though, had already shown up in 1984.
Breed cats whose bodies change color near radiation.
Then attach a song: "If the cat changes color, run."
A chorus you can't shake, they figured, could last 10,000 years.
And in 2014, that song actually got made.
So that after every language changes, "don't dig here" is what's left.

- [줄 1·2] Warning messages at nuclear waste repositories are an attempt to stop humans from digging in on a 10,000-year timescale — 확인 / 기사 도입부
- [줄 2] The U.S. site is the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (Chihuahuan Desert southeast of Carlsbad, New Mexico; salt beds roughly half a mile underground), so "under the desert" is accurate — 확인 / https://www.energy.gov/em/waste-isolation-pilot-plant-wipp
- [줄 2] "10,000 years" is not when the danger ends — it is the regulatory period under EPA 40 CFR 191 and the design period for the markers (Pu-239 half-life is about 24,100 years) — 정정 반영: "at least 10,000 years" / https://www.epa.gov/radiation/environmental-radiation-protection-standards-management-and-disposal-spent-nuclear-fuel
- [줄 3] Today's written languages are unlikely to survive that long, which is why the team examined pictograms and hostile architecture alongside text — 확인 / 기사 "Written messages"
- [줄 4] Nuclear semiotics began with the 1981 Human Interference Task Force (U.S. DOE and Bechtel), whose members included not only engineers and physicists but linguists, anthropologists and semioticians. Linguist Thomas Sebeok joined as a consultant in 1981 — 확인 / https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Interference_Task_Force
- [줄 5] A 1993 Sandia National Laboratories report proposed sample wording for a message to future visitors — 확인 / 기사 "Message", 각주 1(Trauth, Hora, Guzowski, 1993)
- [줄 6] "This place is not a place of honor... no highly esteemed deed is commemorated here... nothing valued is here." — 확인 / 기사 "Message"
- [줄 7] The original reads "What is here was dangerous and repulsive to us," followed by "The danger is still present, in your time" — 정정 반영: "what was here" → "what is here" / 기사 "Message"
- [줄 8] The report examined physical marker designs meant to convey dangerous emanations, forms suggesting bodily harm, and the idea of shunned land that looks destroyed or contaminated — 확인 / 기사 "Physical markers"
- [줄 9] Leaning Stone Spikes: 70 feet (21 m) tall, 15–20 feet wide at the base, leaning 1:14. The figures are correct, but "jutting in every direction" belongs to Landscape of Thorns, which has no published dimensions — the two designs had been mixed — 정정 반영: "at a lean" / https://wipp.energy.gov/pdfs/site_markers.pdf
- [줄 10] Forbidding Blocks: hundreds of black-dyed, house-sized stone blocks (about 25 feet on a side) set in an irregular grid, creating "streets" that lead nowhere — 확인 / 기사 "Physical markers"
- [줄 11] The WIPP information center concept: a 40×32×10-foot granite and concrete structure designed so that wind passing through it produces a "dissonant and mournful" whistling (a Level I message) — 확인 / 기사 "Written messages"
- [줄 12] The ray cat proposal dates to 1984, predating Sandia's 1993 landscape designs. The earlier phrasing "the proposals kept getting stranger" reversed the chronology — 정정 반영: "already in 1984" / https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_cat
- [줄 13] Françoise Bastide and Paolo Fabbri proposed in *Zeitschrift für Semiotik* in 1984 that "ray cats" be bred to change color dramatically near radiation emissions — 확인 / 기사 "Cultural research"
- [줄 14] The plan was to plant the cats' meaning in collective memory through folktales and myth and pass it down in poetry, music and images. The quoted line in the script is an illustrative rendering of that intent, not a source quotation — 확인 / 같은 절
- [줄 15·16] Commissioned by the podcast 99% Invisible, musician Emperor X released "10,000-Year Earworm to Discourage Settlement Near Nuclear Waste Repositories (Don't Change Color, Kitty)" in 2014, designed to be catchy enough to be handed down across generations for 10,000 years — 확인 / https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/ten-thousand-years/
