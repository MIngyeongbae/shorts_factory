# The country where one bridge changes your electricity

- 시드: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/商用電源周波数
- 매체 적합성: Fits — the east–west color split on a map of Japan, a river cutting through the middle of a town, and power squeezing through four narrow converter stations all carry the explanation
- 핵심 질문: Why does one country run two kinds of electricity, and why hasn't it been merged yet?
- 핵심 반전: The line wasn't drawn by terrain or policy — it hardened out of two companies buying a German and an American generator in the 1890s
- 보이는 것: the east–west color fields split at the Fuji River, one town on both banks, the 2011 blackouts in the east beside spare power in the west, and the four narrow converter stations between them
- 차별점: this isn't a border — a river cuts through the middle of a single town, and the left bank and right bank run on different electricity

## 대본

There's a city where crossing a bridge changes your electricity
In Shizuoka, Japan, the Fuji River runs right through town
East of the river, 50 hertz. West, 60
Hertz is how many times a second the current swings
Two songs on different beats — they just won't mix
So how did one country end up with two beats?
In 1895, Tokyo Electric Light imported a German generator
Its beat was 50 hertz, and that became Tokyo's standard
Osaka Electric Light bought American: 60 hertz
The two grew, swallowing towns, and met at that line
No mountain range drew it — two generators did
In the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake, that line became a wall
The west had power to spare, but barely any crossed
Even today, only four stations nationwide can convert that beat
So why not just switch everything to 60 hertz?
The government ran the numbers: 10 trillion yen, 40-plus years
Japan chose to add converter stations instead of unifying
Cross one bridge over the Fuji River, and 1895 is still running

## 주장

- [줄 1~2] In Fuji City and Fujinomiya City, Shizuoka Prefecture, the Fuji River — the boundary of commercial power frequency — runs through the middle of town, leaving 50Hz on the left bank and 60Hz on the right — 확인 「50 Hzと60 Hzの境界線」
- [줄 3] The most famous boundary is the Fuji River in Shizuoka Prefecture; east of the river is 50Hz, west is 60Hz — 확인 「50 Hzと60 Hzの境界線」
- [줄 4] Commercial power frequency means the frequency of the AC supply delivered as mains power — the number of oscillations per second — 확인, definition section of the article
- [줄 5] Grids running at different frequencies cannot exchange power directly; it must pass through a 50Hz↔60Hz frequency converter station — 확인 「東西間での周波数変換による相互融通」
- [줄 6~8] Tokyo Electric Light decided to switch to AC, brought in a 50Hz German AEG generator, and **began operating the Asakusa thermal power station on September 20, 1895** (1893 is the year construction started); the Kanto region was then standardized on 50Hz AC — 정정 반영 「浅草火力発電所」/「東京電燈」. The seed text's "operating in 1893" confuses groundbreaking with operation
- [줄 7 배경] Asakusa housed four domestic Ishikawajima Shipyard 200kW single-phase AC units (2,000V, 100Hz) alongside two German Allgemeine (now AEG) 265kW three-phase units (3,000V, 50Hz) — 확인 「浅草火力発電所」(unused in the script, reversal material)
- [줄 9] Osaka Electric Light adopted American 60Hz generators — 확인 「日本の商用電源周波数」. However, "GE-made 60Hz from its 1888 founding" is 정정: at its 1889 opening it used Thomson-Houston 1,150V AC, and 60Hz at 2,300V settled in between the late 1890s and 1900 「大阪電灯」
- [줄 10] Power supply across the country gradually consolidated around these two poles, producing the east–west frequency divide — 확인 「日本の商用電源周波数」
- [줄 11] The boundary generally follows the Itoigawa–Shizuoka Tectonic Line, but the cause is not terrain — it is the generator choices made by Tokyo Electric Light and Osaka Electric Light in the Meiji era (terrain matches by coincidence; causation is the generators) — 확인 「50 Hzと60 Hzの境界線」/「日本の商用電源周波数」
- [줄 12~13] The 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and the resulting plant shutdowns caused a power shortage, and TEPCO ran rolling blackouts; the differing east–west frequencies, which limited how much power could be transferred, were one cause of the shortfall. Conversion capacity then totaled 1 million kW across three sites (Sakuma 300,000, Shin-Shinano 600,000, Higashi-Shimizu 100,000), against a TEPCO shortfall projected at roughly 10 million kW — 확인 「東西間での周波数変換による相互融通」/「佐久間周波数変換所」
- [줄 14] The frequency converter stations used for inter-utility transfers are four: Sakuma, Shin-Shinano, Higashi-Shimizu, and Hida (Shin-Shinano), with maximum east–west transfer of 2.1 million kW as of March 2021. As of 2026 there are still only four, and the expansion to 3 million kW is under construction with a target of fiscal 2027 — 확인 「東西間での周波数変換による相互融通」/METI materials
- [줄 15~16] Post-2011 government simulations estimated that converting all 50Hz areas to 60Hz would cost roughly 10 trillion yen in equipment replacement and take over 40 years of frequency-conversion work on generating facilities, and the Japanese government judged frequency unification to be unrealistic — 확인 「日本の商用電源周波数」 and METI estimate (March 2012)
- [줄 17] Linking the grids through converter stations instead of unifying them was judged realistic: Higashi-Shimizu began full operation at 300,000kW in February 2013 and Hida-Shinano at 900,000kW in March 2021, raising transfer capacity from 1.2 million kW to 2.1 million kW — 확인 「東西間での周波数変換による相互融通」
- [줄 18] The start of operation at the Asakusa thermal power station in 1895 (Tokyo Electric Light, 50Hz) is the origin of today's boundary — 정정 반영 「浅草火力発電所」
