# Why Does Christmas in Japan Smell Like Chicken?

- 시드: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本ケンタッキー・フライド・チキン
- 매체 적합성: Suitable — American and Japanese Christmas tables, a kindergarten event, and a TV interview show how the custom began and spread.
- 핵심 질문: Why did eating KFC at Christmas become a tradition in Japan?
- 핵심 반전: It’s often seen as an American tradition, but a lie told on TV by a Japanese KFC manager helped it spread.
- 보이는 것: A contrast between turkey dinners and KFC lines, followed by a kindergarten Santa event, a TV interview, and the rise of a nationwide custom.
- 차별점: Instead of tracing the custom to an ad slogan, this follows a lie admitted by the man himself and the convergence of several possible origins.

## 대본
Japan’s Christmas KFC—did this tradition really come from America?
But to Americans, this scene looks more strange than festive.
So where did Japan’s long KFC lines actually begin?
Around 1973, a Christian kindergarten in Aoyama made a request.
They’d buy chicken if someone played Santa at their Christmas party.
The manager, Takeshi Okawara, put on the Santa suit himself.
The children loved it, and word of mouth brought orders from other schools.
Then, a few years later, a TV station asked an unexpected question.
Did Americans eat fried chicken at Christmas too?
Okawara said yes, then later admitted it was a deliberate lie.
But we can’t say that one answer started everything.
Another story says a foreign customer bought KFC after failing to find turkey.
And Japan featured Christmas chicken dishes even before KFC arrived.
So advertising didn’t invent a custom from nothing—it tied scattered pieces together.
And at the center was a fake American tradition created by a store manager.
