## 대본
Why does hanafuda show flowers instead of numbers?
They weren’t there just to make the cards look pretty.
In the late 16th century, Portuguese cards reached Japan.
When gambling spread through them, the Edo shogunate repeatedly banned them.
By the late 18th century, it cracked down on sellers and makers alike.
But educational poem cards such as Hyakunin Isshu were allowed.
Gambling cards were blocked, while poem cards had a way through.
Makers are thought to have pushed flower designs through that narrow opening.
They even disguised some ordinary cards by adding old poems.
The earlier cards had 12 cards in each of 4 suits.
Hanafuda flipped that into 4 cards for each of 12 months, hiding the numbers.
It kept the same cabinet, but changed the labels on its drawers.
That meant the old card games could still be played.
By the end of the 18th century, these flower cards were commercialized in Kyoto.
They were sold as Musashino, an early form of today’s hanafuda.
So hanafuda’s flowers were more than decoration.
They’re the trace of forbidden numbers dressed up as flowers.
